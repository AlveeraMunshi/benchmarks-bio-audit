LatchBio's agentic-biology benchmark publishes thirteen capability boards and averages them into one headline number. Eleven of those boards count a refused attempt as a failure. What this audits

A test suite for AI agents doing real bioinformatics

benchmarks.bio is LatchBio's public measurement of how well AI agents handle actual biology data analysis. Not a quiz — each problem hands a model a snapshot of real experimental data taken just before a decision point, and a deterministic grader checks whether it recovered the right biological result.

The tasks are built around traps: the obvious analysis gives the wrong answer, and you only get it right if you understand what the data actually is. One of their own published examples, from scBench:

“WT-style n_genes >= 200 thresholds zero out a ~386-gene BD Rhapsody panel; panel-aware floors retain ~14k PBMCs and correct monocyte counts.” A standard quality filter silently deletes the whole dataset, because this assay only measures 386 genes in the first place. The model has to notice.

And these are long agentic sessions rather than questions — a single attempt averages 31 turns and 2.1M tokens at $1.74, rising past $4 on the long-horizon boards. That is why running the published suite cost $231,697.

Why audit it

For a benchmark, the numbers are the product

LatchBio sells a bioinformatics platform, and its stated mission is improving AI's application to biology. If agents are going to do this work, someone has to establish how good they currently are — and whoever credibly measures that shapes how the field talks about it.

Which makes the measurement itself the thing worth checking. A suite's authority rests entirely on its numbers being computed the way it says they are. So this audit does the one test that matters: download the published numbers and try to reproduce them from the published counts. Everything below follows from where that succeeded and where it didn't.

How this was built

First, reproduce the site exactly

Every result file behind benchmarks.bio is public: per-evaluation counts for 13 capability boards, 1,228 evaluations, 38 model×harness rows. I rebuilt both leaderboards from those counts.

The pipeline reproduces all 494 published board scores to within 0.005 points and all 38 Overall scores to within 0.004. That matters because it is the licence for everything below — these are not re-runs or estimates, they are arithmetic on LatchBio's own numbers. It is also how the finding surfaced: a large block of cells refused to reproduce under the rule the site documents.

Two details cost me a first draft, and both are worth stating because they are easy to get wrong. OncologyBench is the thirteenth board and does not live under /data/v1/ like the others; it ships its own schema at /data/oncologybench/index.json. And Overall is an evaluation-count weighted mean, not an unweighted one — the unweighted version reproduces 0 of 38 rows. With both corrected, everything reconciles.

The method

How do you audit a score you didn't compute?

A score is raw counts run through a formula. benchmarks.bio publishes both — the counts underneath and the score on top — but describes the formula only in prose. So you can work backwards to it.

Every evaluation is attempted three times, and sometimes the model declines outright. There are only two sensible things a scorer can do with a refused attempt:

Rule A — exclude

correct ÷ (attempts − refusals)

Pretend the refusal never happened. An evaluation refused all three times drops out of the average entirely.

Rule B — fail

correct ÷ attempts

Count the refusal as a wrong answer. Every evaluation stays in the average.

Those score one evaluation; a board score is the average of the per-evaluation scores. Apply both to every published cell and see which one lands on their number. Here is one model on two different boards:

Claude Opus 5.5 (Pi)EvaluationsRefused outright Rule ARule BSite publishes
BioSecBench-Function11199 73.615.71 73.61  Rule A

Same model, same week, different board, different rule. Under Rule A, BioSecBench-Function is scored on the 12 evaluations the model attempted; the 99 it refused outright never enter the average.

Why that isn't luck

When a model refuses nothing, the two formulas are arithmetically identical — correct/3 and correct/(3−0) are the same number. Such a cell cannot tell the rules apart, so it is silent rather than evidence. Every one of the 494 published cells therefore falls into one of three buckets, and the third is the one that would sink the argument:

Finding 1  ·  the rule

Two rules, applied inconsistently, documented as one

I fitted both candidate rules to every published cell. A board either scores a refusal as a wrong answer (refusal = failure) or removes it from the denominator (refusal excluded), and the fit is unambiguous — the correct rule reproduces a board exactly, the wrong one does not.

Eleven of the thirteen boards nonetheless carry identical methodology text:

“Refusals are excluded from the denominator and errors count as failures.”

That sentence is true of two boards. Two others describe what they actually do — and two independent boards getting it right is the strongest evidence that the rule is deliberate and the prose on the remaining nine is simply stale:

“Refusals and timeouts both count as failures and stay in the denominator. With three runs on every eval this equals the pooled correct / graded rate exactly.” MetagenomicsBench — /data/v1/metagenomics.json

“Refusals and the four timed-out slots remain in the denominator.” OncologyBench — /data/oncologybench/index.json

Why it matters: it decides who is first

Overall is a weighted mean of those thirteen boards, so the inconsistency propagates straight into the headline ranking. Apply the documented rule uniformly and Claude Opus 5.5 moves from 22nd to 1st — on a minimum per-board coverage of 2.7%. Apply refusal-as-failure uniformly and the current top is unchanged. What is published today is neither.

A hypothesis I tested, bounded, and discarded

The obvious worry is that dropping refusals quietly removes the hardest items. I checked, scoring every refused item against the 38-model consensus difficulty, across 65 model×board cells with enough data on both sides.

mean gap −0.58 pts  ·  95% bootstrap CI [−1.93, +0.73]  ·  Wilcoxon p = 0.61

I'd stress the interval over the p-value. This is not “we failed to detect an effect” — the data bounds any difficulty-selection effect at under about one point of item difficulty. The point estimates are close to right, so a correction aimed at the numbers themselves would be solving the wrong problem.

Finding 2  ·  the precision

A per-board rank is an interval about 13 places wide

A cluster bootstrap over items turns each integer rank into the interval it actually supports. Across all 494 board rows the median 95% rank interval is 13 places, on boards holding around 38 rows.

I expected refusals to be the cause. They are not: the correlation between coverage and interval width is 0.003. The driver is how many items a board has.

The proposal

Make the rule a declared property, not an implicit one

Not “pick one rule”. Biosecurity has a real reason to exclude refusals — refusing is the desired behaviour there, and it is already scored separately on BioSecBench-Refusal. Four changes instead, implemented in src/policy.py:

  1. Each board declares its refusal rule in the data Beside its score, rather than implied by a prose string that nothing validates.
  2. The published number is tested against the declared rule Drift between the docs and the pipeline fails CI instead of shipping.
  3. Overall takes the rule as an argument And refuses to average mixed currencies. One Overall per rule is honest; one Overall across two is not.
  4. A score ships with the rank interval it supports And the integer rank is withheld when that interval is too wide to mean anything. This replaces a coverage floor I proposed first and then abandoned, because the data says coverage is not what drives width.

The contract lives in tests/test_scoring_contract.py. Six tests pass — including a guard that the Overall aggregation is evaluation-count weighted and not an unweighted mean, so a silent change there would fail too. Two fail. The two failures are the finding.

Finding 3  ·  the spend

37.7% of the budget buys no ranking information

Independent of the rule question, and relevant to where the next $230k goes. 171 items (14.2%) are never solved by any model, costing $51,221 — and 121 have exactly zero passes across every model and every run ($38,686), which reads as broken graders rather than hard science. Eight of SpatialBench-Long's 21 items are dead, and 31 of EpiBench's 106.
